A plain-language research note

Could the search for energy help explain the rise of intelligence?

A star gives a planet a steady supply of energy. Life finds ways to use some of it. Could the ability to find and use energy help explain why living things develop ways to sense, predict and act?

Imagine standing beside a waterfall. Water high up has the potential to do useful work as it falls. A waterwheel can capture some of that opportunity. The water does not disappear; it continues downstream, but the difference in height that made it useful has been reduced.

The universe contains energy differences on a much grander scale. A star is extremely hot. Space is extremely cold. Energy flows from the hot star into the colder surroundings. A planet intercepts a little of that flow. And on at least one planet, some of that energy is captured by living things.

This raises a question: could the evolution of life, and eventually some forms of intelligence, be understood partly as the evolution of ways to access and use available energy?

That is the idea behind the Dissipative Agency simulation. It is a hypothesis to investigate, not a claim that the universe has a plan or that intelligence is guaranteed to appear.

First, what do “entropy” and “dissipation” mean?

In school science, we learn that energy cannot be created or destroyed; it changes form. We also learn that when energy changes form, some of its ability to do useful work is often lost. A moving object slows down because of friction. Its organised motion is spread into tiny, random motions of molecules: heat.

Entropy is a physics quantity that helps describe this spreading and the number of microscopic arrangements compatible with what we observe. It is often introduced as “disorder”, but that is only a rough shortcut. For our purpose, think of entropy as one way physicists keep track of how energy spreads out and becomes less available to do useful work.

Dissipation is the process through which a concentrated energy difference is used up or spread out. A hot cup of tea cools because energy flows from the tea into the room. The tea becomes less hot; the room receives the energy. The total entropy of the tea and its surroundings increases.

The second law of thermodynamics says that the total entropy of an isolated system does not decrease. It does not say that every system becomes less organised at every moment. A living cell can build an organised structure and maintain it, provided it takes in energy and releases heat and waste to its surroundings. Local organisation can increase while the surroundings gain more entropy.

Why bring life into this picture?

Earth receives energy from the Sun and sends energy back into space. Plants capture a fraction of incoming sunlight and store some of it in chemical form. Animals obtain chemical energy by eating plants or other animals. Cells use energy to maintain themselves, repair damage, move things around and reproduce. Much of the energy eventually ends up as heat.

Life is therefore not an alternative to the flow of energy. It is one set of processes that takes part in that flow. It captures some energy temporarily, uses it to maintain complex structures, and passes energy onward in altered forms.

Now consider an environment with uneven resources. Some places may receive more sunlight than others; some may remain favourable for longer; some may change unpredictably. A simple organism that happens to occupy a rich patch may obtain more energy. But another organism that can detect a better patch and move there might do better still.

That suggests a possible evolutionary path. A basic ability to detect conditions could be useful. Processing information could help separate a meaningful signal from noise. Making a rough prediction could help when conditions change. Planning could help an organism choose between an immediate reward and a future opportunity. Movement or another form of action could let it exploit that choice.

These abilities have costs. Sensing and processing require physical machinery. Movement uses energy. Prediction can be wrong. A capability will only spread through evolution when its benefits, on average, outweigh its costs in the environment where it is used.

What exactly are we testing?

The simulation represents a planet as a grid of patches. Each patch receives a changing amount of usable energy. A population of simplified entities competes to capture a limited share. The entities can inherit traits, vary through mutation, survive and reproduce according to their energy balance.

These model entities are not meant to represent individual bacteria, animals or species exactly. They are simplified units that let us ask how certain mechanisms behave. The model also includes traits for collecting energy, cooperation and technology-like capacity, but the experiment discussed here focuses on the information-and-action package.

An earlier version gave that package a direct bonus in the equation for harvesting capacity. That was a problem: it was like saying that a better thinker automatically gets a bigger bucket, then announcing that better thinking leads to more water. The result was partly built into the rules.

For the new experiment, that direct bonus was removed. The size of an entity's harvesting capacity now depends on its collector, technology, cooperation and shared infrastructure. The information-related traits can influence only what the entity observes, what it predicts, how it weighs a forecast, and whether it moves towards a promising nearby patch. They still have costs.

We tested two environments. In one, energy-rich patches were strong and reasonably predictable. In the other, the hotspots shifted more erratically. In each environment, we changed how noisy the information was: low, medium or high. For comparison, each condition had a control population in which sensing, information processing, prediction, planning and deliberate action were switched off. The control could still improve its collecting, cooperation and technology-like traits.

What happened?

The five information-and-action traits did not disappear when the direct harvesting bonus was removed. They evolved to non-zero average values in the cognitive-enabled runs. More importantly, the entities in those runs captured more energy per entity on average than the no-cognition controls.

Across the 36 matched pairs of runs, the cognitive-enabled treatment had higher captured power per entity in 35. When the six repeats were averaged in each condition, the per-entity advantage was positive in all six environment-and-noise combinations.

But there is a second result, and it changes the interpretation.

The cognitive-enabled populations were smaller and captured less energy in total. In all six condition averages, their total biological energy throughput was lower than that of the no-cognition control. For example, in the strong, predictable environment with low noise, the cognitive-enabled population averaged about 2,939 model entities, compared with about 8,508 in the control. It captured about 74.5 terawatts in total, compared with 134.8 terawatts. Yet each cognitive-enabled entity captured about 32.3 gigawatts on average, roughly twice the control's 16.0 gigawatts.

Why can both things be true? Picture a field with a limited number of rich feeding spots. A population that can identify and move towards those spots may concentrate around them. The individuals that reach the rich spots get more. But crowding and competition mean fewer individuals can be supported, and much of the wider field may be left unused. The total collected by the whole population can fall even as the average for each remaining individual rises.

That is broadly what the model shows. The cognitive-enabled populations move more and occupy a smaller fraction of the grid. They become more concentrated. The energy advantage is real within the model, but it is an advantage to the individual entity, not to the population as a whole.

Does this prove that intelligence increases entropy?

No. That would be a much bigger claim than this experiment can support.

First, the experiment tests a combined package. Better sensing, processing, prediction and planning work together with action-driven movement. We have not yet separated the value of better information from the value of being able to move around. Perhaps movement alone explains much of the advantage. Perhaps information makes movement more effective. Perhaps the combination matters. The next experiment must hold one part fixed while changing the other.

Second, capturing energy is not the same measurement as producing entropy. Captured energy can be stored temporarily, transformed into chemical energy, used for maintenance, or released as heat. To calculate entropy production properly, we would need to keep a much more complete account of those processes and of the planet's atmosphere, surface and radiation.

Third, the simulation is a simplified world. It does not create life from non-living chemistry. It begins with a population that already exists. It does not include a realistic food web, predators, nutrient cycles or detailed climate. And its estimate of planetary entropy depends strongly on a simplified assumption about how biological cover changes the planet's reflectivity.

The careful conclusion: this experiment supports a limited possibility: information and movement capabilities can evolve in this model even when we stop giving them a direct harvesting bonus. But the resulting strategy benefits the individual entities while reducing the population's total energy capture. The experiment does not show that intelligence maximises planetary dissipation.

What should we test next?

The next test is a more careful separation of information and movement. We should compare at least four cases:

Information and directed movement: entities receive noisy signals and can use those signals to choose where to move. This is the combined mechanism tested so far.

Information without directed movement: entities can sense, process and predict, but cannot move towards a chosen patch. This asks whether information has value on its own in the current model.

Movement without useful information: entities can move, but their movement is random or based on a very weak signal. This tests whether simply exploring more of the landscape explains the result.

Neither: entities do not process the signals and do not deliberately move. This is the baseline control.

We should repeat these comparisons across the same levels of uncertainty, and then vary the energetic costs of sensing and movement. Only then can we say more confidently what part of the apparent advantage comes from information and what part comes from movement.

The larger question remains open

The broad thought behind this project is that the universe contains energy gradients, and living systems are among the structures that have evolved to make use of them. It is tempting to take that thought further and suggest that life, intelligence and technology are natural consequences of the universe's tendency to dissipate energy differences.

But a tendency is not a guarantee. The second law does not tell us that a particular kind of structure must emerge. Local evolution does not automatically optimise the whole planet. A strategy that is successful for one organism can be inefficient for the ecosystem around it.

That is why the simulation is useful only if we allow it to disagree with the story we hoped to tell. The result so far has made the hypothesis narrower and more interesting: the pursuit of local energy access may help select for agency, but local advantage does not necessarily add up to greater global energy dissipation.

The next round of experiments should make that claim harder to sustain—or give it stronger support. Either outcome is progress.

Update, 23 November 2026

The next round came back, and it made the claim narrower again.

When creatures with and without the thinking traits shared one planet, the thinkers won, and the planet used less energy in total. Traits that did nothing at all rose about as far as the thinking traits did. And by generation 660, most of the behaviour had faded away.

A separate test asked whether only life can unlock energy that sits behind a barrier. It cannot. A fire and a large enough mineral surface did it too. What only life did was get better at it.

So the hypothesis now reads like this. Things persist when they use energy well enough to pay their way, living or not. What life adds is improvement. And improvement should matter most when the world keeps changing. That is the next thing to test.
